## The holiday premise: unsafe as written

"A great holiday with a miserable last day is remembered as a bad holiday" is not supported, and the direct evidence runs against a simple version of it.

- **Kemp, Burt & Furneaux (2008):** 49 students on vacation for an average of 7 days sent daily texts on their happiness. Afterward: "The peak-end rule was not an outstandingly good predictor." They could not recall their day-to-day changes in happiness, and the results "indicate much reconstruction of the affective states" (abstract). Vacation length made no difference, which is consistent with duration neglect.
- **Chark, King & Tang (2022; online 2020):** 691 travelers to Macau. "Summary evaluations are better predicted using an arithmetic average of all episodic evaluations, instead of the peak-end rule." The authors suggest travel is longer and more complex than the experiences psychologists have tested (abstract).
- **Strijbosch et al. (2019):** not a holiday; 40 students, a 14-minute VR film. The rule "cannot be generalized to ecologically more valid experiential episodes" and is limited for experiences of a "rich and heterogeneous nature". Both quotes were checked against the article text.
- **Evidence on the other side:** Lin (2025), tourists: peaks, troughs and ends of mood and satisfaction affected post-trip loyalty, "thereby supporting the peak–end rule", and bad extremes may weigh more than good ones (abstract). Yu et al. (2024): the end effect is heavily weighted with an individualistic mindset, less with a collective one, and they call earlier findings "mixed" (abstract).
- **Related, not about endings:** Wirtz et al. (2003): remembered experience, more than the experience felt at the time, predicted wanting to repeat a spring break (abstract).
- **Logic of the rule:** it averages peak and end. A great holiday keeps a high peak, so a bad last day would pull the memory down, not flip it to "bad". The brief's wording goes beyond the rule. No study found shows a holiday reversed by its last day.
- **Limits stated by the authors:** the 1993 paper doesn't claim that peak and end are the only predictors, and suspects memories are sometimes dominated by the first moments (p. 404).

**Recommended change:** move the premise to a short, single episode, such as a dinner, a film, or a meeting that overruns. If a holiday stays, use it as the twist: "For a week-long holiday, the evidence is much weaker." The second version is honest and fits the channel.

## Wrong or weaker versions to avoid

- "One bad ending ruins everything." See above.
- "People forget the rest." The 1993 authors: "We do not claim that other information is necessarily lost, only that it is often not used" (p. 404). Most subjects could still say which trial was longer (p. 404).
- "Everybody chose the longer one." 69%, and only among those who felt relief does it reach 81%.
- "Just one degree warmer" stated as exact. The mean rise was 1.1 °C (pp. 402–403).
- "Participants did the third trial." They were told there would be none after they chose (p. 402).
- "Studied in men and women." The 1993 sample was all male.
- "Patients came back more often after a gentler ending" stated flatly. Return rates were 53% vs 48%, P > 0.20 before adjustment. The odds ratio of 1.41 (95% CI for the change in odds 2% to 96%, P = 0.038) appears only after adjusting for three clinical predictors (p. 190). The authors call the rates "slightly higher" (p. 187).
- "Three extra minutes." The protocol allowed up to 3 minutes; mean length differed by about 1 minute (27.6 vs 26.8, P > 0.20) (pp. 188–189).
- "Memory ignores everything but peak and end." In the 2003 trial, remembered pain correlated more with average pain (r = 0.56) than with peak (r = 0.44) or final pain (r = 0.33) (p. 190).
- Do et al. (2008) as a vacation study. At least one popular site says so. Its abstract concerns material goods, not vacations.
- The dissertation figure r = .57 (https://psychology.gmu.edu/defenses/1259). Use the published r = 0.581.

## Replication status

- **Strong overall:** the 1993 paper reports its own replication, and Alaybek et al. (2022) find a large, robust effect across 174 effect sizes.
- **Qualified by:** the average predicts about as well (Alaybek abstract), and tests on long, complex experiences are mixed or negative (holiday section above).
- **Unknown:** the full text of Alaybek et al. was not accessible, so whether vacations were included, or whether episode length moderates the effect, is not established here. The dissertation's abstract does not mention episode length.

## Where the brief needs correcting

- **Cold-water claim:** correct. "Most" is 22 of 32 (69%), p. 403; male students only.
- **"The colonoscopy follow-up is Redelmeier & Kahneman (1996)":** incomplete. That study is observational: it recorded pain and related it to memory. The 1993 paper already cites this line of work as Redelmeier & Kahneman 1993 (pp. 401–402). The actual intervention is the 2003 randomized trial.
- **Premise:** see above.
- **Turn, "end things well: meetings, dates, visits, a reel":** it is a reasonable reading, but no source read tests a better ending on meetings or dates. Say "the ending can count for more than you'd expect", not "this will work". The strongest applied evidence is one medical trial with modest effects.
- **Health guardrail:** present the colonoscopy trial as a finding about memory. It must not read as medical advice or as a recommendation to prolong a procedure. The 1993 authors themselves call the choice of extra pain "a mistake" (p. 404).
