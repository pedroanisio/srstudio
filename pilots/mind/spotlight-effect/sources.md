The T-shirt finding is about **naming the person on the shirt**. Observers were asked whether they could remember who was pictured (p. 213). "Noticed" is the paper's own word, but on an evidence card "could say who was on the shirt" is the accurate version.

## Wrong or weaker versions to avoid

- "Nobody noticed." 23% could name the person. One in four is not nobody.
- "Nobody is looking at you." The paper states that people "do not always overestimate" and raises a possible reverse effect for habits one has stopped noticing, such as cologne and smoking (p. 220). That reverse effect is the authors' suggestion, not a tested result.
- "Others notice you half as much as you think, always." The doubling is one condition in one study of 15 wearers.
- "Flattering shirts get noticed less than embarrassing ones." 8% vs 23% is not a finding. The authors suspect the observers' more involving questionnaire caused the gap (p. 215, footnote 3).
- "The error was 23 points" presented as the same thing as "23% noticed". Both numbers exist and are different quantities. The mean overestimate was 23% with a 95% CI of 9% to 38% (p. 213); the actual rate was also 23% (p. 215).
- "50% and 25%", which is common in blogs. The paper says 46% and 23%. "About half" and "about a quarter" are fine in words; printed numerals should be 46% and 23%.
- "A lecture hall full of classmates." It was a lab room with 2 to 6 observers seated along one side of a long table (p. 212).
- Any clinical reading. The paper makes no treatment or anxiety claim, and the reel should not either.

## Replication status

- **Within the paper:** Study 2 repeated the T-shirt pattern. Study 5 reused the shirt with a different sample but measured estimates only.
- **Same research group:** Savitsky, Epley & Gilovich (2001), *JPSP 81*(1), 44–56, https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.81.1.44 (abstract). Epley, Savitsky & Gilovich (2002), *JPSP 83*(2), 300–312, https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.83.2.300 (abstract). Gilovich, Kruger & Medvec (2002), *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology 38*(1), 93–99, https://doi.org/10.1006/jesp.2001.1490 (citation confirmed; abstract and text not read).
- **Independent, conceptual:** Brown & Stopa (2007), *Journal of Anxiety Disorders 21*(6), 804–819, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.janxdis.2006.11.006 (abstract), found a stronger spotlight effect under social evaluation. Boothby, Clark & Bargh (2017), *JPSP 112*(4), 589–606, https://doi.org/10.1037/pspi0000082 (abstract), is a neighboring result: people believe they are watched less than they watch others.
- **Direct replication:** web searches found no preregistered direct replication of the T-shirt study and no meta-analysis. They also found no failed replication. This was not a search of registries, so it does not show that none exists.
- One blog says the effect "replicated across cultures, paradigms, populations, and decades" (https://atticusli.com/replication-crisis/spotlight-effect/) and names no independent study. Do not repeat that.

Summary for the evidence card: a well-cited effect with supporting follow-ups, resting on small samples, mostly from the original group. Say "in one study", not "science shows".

## Where the brief needs correcting

- **Figures:** "about half" and "roughly a quarter" are right. Exact values are 46% and 23%, Study 1, p. 215 footnote 3 and Fig. 1.
- **Premise, "Nobody else noticed":** contradicted by the 23%.
- **Premise, "a whole day" with "a small stain or a bad haircut":** not what was tested. Wearers entered a room briefly, once (pp. 212–213). The authors only "suspect" the effect covers a new haircut (p. 214). Stains are not mentioned. The closest evidence on everyday appearance is claim 9.
- **Turn, "far less than you think":** safer as "less than you think".
- **Guardrail conflict:** the shirt showed the real singer Barry Manilow (p. 212), and Study 2 used Bob Marley, Jerry Seinfeld and Martin Luther King, Jr. (p. 214). The original-only rule bars showing real people, so the reel should describe it as "an embarrassing T-shirt" and draw an invented image.
