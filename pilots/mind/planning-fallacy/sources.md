"Days" means days from the survey call to the finish, not the total time spent on the thesis (p. 369).

## Wrong or weaker versions to avoid

- "Use last time's number and you'll be right." See the turn below.
- "Students thought a thesis takes a month; it took two." The clock started at the survey call, in their final semester, not at the start of the thesis.
- "Everyone always underestimates." A large review found underestimation was reported more often than overestimation in engineering and management studies, but "this was not the case in studies from the psychology literature" (Halkjelsvik & Jørgensen 2012, abstract).
- "Your friend is lazy" or anything that makes it a character flaw. The paper explains the bias mainly by what people focus on when they predict (Table 3, p. 373; p. 379), so it gives no support for that reading.
- "Thinking about the worst case fixes it." Pessimistic estimates were no more accurate (p. 369).
- "Planning fallacy = big tasks are underestimated and small tasks overestimated." The review questions that pattern as partly a statistical artifact (Halkjelsvik & Jørgensen 2012, abstract).

## The turn: "estimate from how long it took last time"

This is only partly supported, and the plain version is contradicted by the paper's own text.

- **What supports it:** Study 4, claims 10 and 11. The authors: people "make more realistic completion estimates when they use their past experiences to inform their predictions" (p. 376).
- **What undercuts it:**
  - Simply recalling the past did nothing (p. 376, claim 9).
  - The change did not make estimates more accurate, only less optimistic: "less biased but no more accurate" (p. 375).
  - It did not make anyone finish sooner: actual completion did not differ (p. 375).
  - Students' typical past times did not correlate with their predictions, so they were not really using "last time" (p. 375).
  - Using last time directly would have been "far too pessimistic" in this study (p. 376).
  - The authors' own summary: "we do not yet have compelling evidence that individuals can use their recollections of their own past performances to improve the accuracy of their time estimates" (p. 380).
- **A further problem with memory:** Roy, Christenfeld & McKenzie (2005) review evidence that people remember past tasks as shorter than they were (abstract). "How long it took last time" may itself be too short. Buehler and colleagues published a comment on that paper in the same issue (*Psychological Bulletin 131*(5), 757–760); it was not read.
- **Reference-class forecasting:** Flyvbjerg (2006) bases forecasts on the record of comparable projects. His first practical case is cost forecasts for large transport infrastructure (abstract), so it does not transfer directly to a friend getting ready.

**Wording the evidence can carry:** "Before you say a time, picture how it went the last few times and how this one could go the same way." Claim the result as "the optimism went away", not "you'll be accurate". A worked evidence card: 5.5 predicted vs 6.8 actual in the control group; 7.0 vs 7.0 in the group that linked past to present (Table 4, p. 375).

## Replication status

- **Within the paper:** Studies 1 to 4 all found the bias, from about 1 day (Studies 3 and 4) to several weeks (Study 1) (p. 379). Study 2 replicated the bias and extended it to everyday tasks (p. 371).
- **Later reviews:** Halkjelsvik & Jørgensen (2012), *Psychological Bulletin 138*(2), 238–271, https://doi.org/10.1037/a0025996 (abstract). Buehler, Griffin & Peetz (2010), *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology 43*, 1–62, https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(10)43001-4 (citation confirmed; not read).
- **Direct replication:** web searches found no preregistered direct replication of the thesis study or of Study 4, and no failed one. This was not a search of registries.
- **Debiasing claims not usable yet:** Kruger & Evans (2004), *JESP 40*(5), 586–598, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2003.11.001, says unpacking a task into steps helps; only the title was confirmed. A search result says Buehler, Griffin & Peetz (2010) mostly eliminated the bias with an obstacles exercise; **secondary source only**, do not print.

## Where the brief needs correcting

- **Figures:** right in rough terms. Exact values are 33.9 and 55.5 days, Study 1, p. 369, Table 1. "A month" and "nearly two" work only as "about 34 days, then about 56". Those are days from the survey.
- **Turn:** "estimate from how long it took last time" overclaims. Use the wording above.
- **Premise, "Quick task, ten minutes":** the paper measured when tasks were finished, in days, not how long the work took. Thesis, Study 2 and Study 4 outcomes are all completion dates. Halkjelsvik & Jørgensen (2012) say many studies fail to separate these two kinds of prediction (abstract). None of the papers read tests a ten-minute job. The vignette is fine as recognition, but the card must not imply the thesis study showed it.
- **Useful for the recipient trigger:** the friend's guess about you is wrong too, in the other direction (claim 12). The authors expect that not to hold every time (p. 380).
