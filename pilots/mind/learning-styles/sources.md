## "Match the format to the material"

It has a real source, Willingham (2005). That source is an expert column by a cognitive scientist, not a controlled trial of the claim, so it should be stated as advice from a researcher (his example is teaching what the Mayan pyramids look like: "Everyone should see the picture."), not as a measured effect. Pashler et al. may also address this in the full text, but that could not be confirmed, so do not attribute it to them.

**Strongest turn:** "Test yourself instead of rereading" (claims 14 to 16). It has the best evidence and is a concrete action. The format-to-material idea can serve as a secondary line, attributed to Willingham.

Dual coding, the idea that verbal and visual memory work as separate systems, comes from Clark & Paivio (1991), citation confirmed, not read. Dunlosky et al. did not rate dual coding. They rated "imagery use for text learning", and rated it low (abstract). Do not write "dual coding per Dunlosky". Clinton-Lisell & Litzinger (2024) recommend multimodal presentation, such as text with visuals, for everyone (full text).

## Wrong or weaker versions to avoid

- "Learning styles have been disproved." Pashler et al.: "it would be an error to conclude that all possible versions of learning styles have been tested and found wanting; many have simply not been tested at all" (abstract). Say "no good evidence that matching helps".
- "Matching has zero effect." See the next section. One meta-analysis found a small benefit.
- "Visual learners don't exist." Preferences and aptitudes differ (claim 7). The myth is that matching teaching to them improves learning.
- "89% of people believe this." The figure covers educators, not the public, and comes mostly from convenience samples.
- "Everyone is told this at school." No source located measures it.
- Pashler "found one study supporting and two contradicting." This appears in a secondary source. **Secondary source only**; do not print.
- The year: Pashler is sometimes cited as 2009, as in Rogowsky et al. 2020. PubMed gives December 2008, volume 9, issue 3. Use 2008.

## Replication status

- **Mostly consistent:** Rogowsky et al. 2015 and 2020 tested the design Pashler prescribed and found no support for meshing. Cuevas (2015) found that the more sound studies "have tended to refute the hypothesis" (abstract). Hattie & O'Leary (2025) report d = .04 for matching studies (abstract).
- **The one complication:** Clinton-Lisell & Litzinger (2024), 21 studies, 101 effect sizes, 1,712 participants, found an overall benefit, g = 0.31, 95% CI 0.05 to 0.57, p = 0.02 (the Results section gives g = 0.32, CI 0.07 to 0.57). Only 26% of outcome measures showed the crossover Pashler requires, most of those studies did not meet quality standards, and the authors call the benefits "too small and too infrequent to warrant widespread adoption". They "advise extreme caution" in using it to justify matching. Heterogeneity was high (I² = 91.17).

A reversal reel must stay accurate given that result: "the evidence doesn't support teaching to your style" holds; "matching does nothing" does not.

## Where the brief needs correcting

- **Evidence:** "no adequate evidence that teaching to a person's preferred style improves learning" is a fair paraphrase. The paper's words are about evidence for "incorporating learning-styles assessments into general educational practice" (abstract).
- **Label "myth":** fine for the claim that matching improves learning; too strong for the whole concept, per the authors' own caveat.
- **Premise, "Everyone was told":** say "many of us", or show one character being told.
- **Turn:** "matching the format to the material" is supportable as expert advice, not as a tested finding. Retrieval practice is the safer lead.
- **Page:** only p. 105 has any confirmation, and that is secondary. **Full text remains to be checked by someone with journal access.**
